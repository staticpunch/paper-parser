## In brief

Susanto et al. developed a tag-free method to characterize ribosomeassociated proteins from any sample. They identified hundreds of candidate ribosome-associated proteins, some of which were dynamic across embryonic tissues or during responses to cell stimuli. These included proteins that may be important for neural development or during immune activation.

## Resource

## SUMMARY

Ribosomes are emerging as direct regulators of gene expression, with ribosome-associated proteins (RAPs) allowing ribosomes to modulate translation. Nevertheless, a lack of technologies to enrich RAPs across sample types has prevented systematic analysis of RAP identities, dynamics, and functions. We have developed a label-free methodology called RAPIDASH to enrich ribosomes and RAPs from any sample. We applied RAPIDASH to mouse embryonic tissues and identified hundreds of potential RAPs, including Dhx30 and Llph, two forebrain RAPs important for neurodevelopment. We identified a critical role of LLPH in neural development linked to the translation of genes with long coding sequences. In addition, we showed that RAPIDASH can identify ribosome changes in cancer cells. Finally, we characterized ribosome composition remodeling during immune cell activation and observed extensive changes post-stimulation. RAPIDASH has therefore enabled the discovery of RAPs in multiple cell types, tissues, and stimuli and is adaptable to characterize ribosome remodeling in several contexts.

## INTRODUCTION

Recently, ribosomes have emerged as direct regulators of gene expression. Interestingly, ribosome-associated proteins (RAPs) such as RNA-binding proteins allow for specificity and regulation over mRNA translation. A handful of RAPs have been further functionally characterized, such as methionine aminopeptidase (MetAP), 1 fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein (RNP) 1 protein (FMRP), 2-4 pyruvate kinase M (PKM), 5,6 RNAbinding protein with multiple splicing (RBPMS), 7 UFM1-specific ligase 1 (UFL1), 5,8 and cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1). 5,9,10 MetAP directly binds to ES27L, 1 a region of the ribosome that has expanded ribosomal RNA (rRNA) known as expansion seg-

ments (ESs), and cleaves the initiator methionine of polypeptides as they are synthesized. Surprisingly, MetAP is important for translation fidelity. 1 FMRP inhibits the translation of neuronal mRNAs. 2-4 PKM directly interacts with ribosomes and acts as a translational activator for a subset of mRNAs. 5,6 RBPMS is an RNA-binding protein that regulates the translation of mRNAs crucial for mesoderm differentiation. 7 In addition to these proteins, post-translational modification enzymes interact with and modify the ribosome. UFL1 is the sole known enzyme that deposits a post-translational modification known as UFMylation on proteins such as Rpl26, which is associated with an increase in the stalling of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) translocon-associated ribosomes. 8,11,12 Lastly, CDK1 phosphorylates Rpl12,

which promotes translation of mitosis-related mRNAs. 9 Thus, RAPs connect the ribosome and mRNA translation to many important cellular functions.

Although RAPs diversify translational control, a lack of methods to rapidly and systematically identify RAPs in different tissue and cell types has stymied our ability to understand fundamental aspects of RAP biology. For example, how many RAPs exist, and do RAPs differ between cell types, tissues, and species? Most prior work has relied on fractionation of cell lysate according to density 13,14 or size 15 to identify RAPs. However, these techniques are highly nonspecific. For example, ER microsomes have heterogeneous densities that are present throughout most fractions, including those containing ribosomes (Figure S1A). Our lab used a genetic approach, which we refer to as RiboFLAG immunoprecipitation (IP), where core ribosomal proteins (RPs) were endogenously tagged with FLAG epitope tag to enable the IP of ribosomes and RAPs for subsequent analysis by mass spectrometry (MS). This enabled the discovery of hundreds of RAPs in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), collectively known as the ribo-interactome. 5 Although Ribo-FLAG IP is exceptionally specific, the reliance on genetic editing hinders the implementation of this strategy in many different sample types, such as tissues or patient samples. A chemical approach, active ribosome capture-MS (ARC-MS), was recently reported as an orthogonal strategy to isolate ribosomes and RAPs. 7 Here, Click chemistry is employed to isolate ribosomes and RAPs for analysis by MS. However, this technique requires treating samples with azidohomoalanine and biases the findings toward ribosomes at the early steps of translation. 7

We sought to develop a methodology that relies on the biophysical properties of the ribosome without genetic tagging or chemical modification, namely, the fact that it contains an rRNA core. In our approach, high-density protein complexes are pelleted by sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation and then un-

dergo chromatography with cysteine-charged sulfolink resin to enrich those that contain RNA, which are mostly ribosomal complexes (Figure 1A). We called this methodology RAP identification by affinity to sulfhydryl-charged resin (RAPIDASH).

Herein, we have used RAPIDASH on mouse embryonic forebrain, limb, and liver tissues and show that ribosomes differ in RAP composition across tissue types. We focused on candidates in the forebrain and characterized Dhx30, Elavl2, and learning-associated protein of slug with 18 kDa (LAPS18)-like protein homolog (Llph) as bona fide RAPs. We further characterized the role of LLPH in human induced neurons, which revealed a critical role of LLPH in neural development and function. In addition, we discovered LLPH has a selective role in translational control, particularly for long transcripts that may require more ribosome-directed control of mRNA translation. We also applied RAPIDASH to characterize RAP dynamics in cancer cells and primary murine macrophages that are treated with different stimuli. This demonstrated the broad applicability of RAPIDASH to many sample types and its ability to characterize dynamic remodeling of ribosome composition.

## RESULTS

## Characterization of RAPIDASH in mESCs and cancer cells

We turned to a chromatographic technique that had been used to isolate active ribosomes from bacteria 17 and yeast 18 by binding of RNA moieties, likely rRNA, to a cysteine-charged resin. While the sulfhydryl-charged resin was used to isolate ribosomes, this protocol did not maintain interactions between ribosomes and RAPs. We made several changes to the protocol developed for yeast ribosomes 18 to optimize the binding and elution of RAP-bound mammalian ribosomes: the centrifugation was performed prior to resin chromatography, the

ultracentrifugation speed and time were altered, and the buffers were changed to maintain interactions with salt-sensitive RAPs. Weisolated ribosomes using the protocol for yeast ribosomes or our optimized RAPIDASH protocol. The peptides derived from the enriched proteins from each sample were labeled with tandemmasstags (TMTs) so the relative levels of the proteins could be quantified by MS. Out of the 212 proteins identified, only 61 proteins gave quantifiable signal in the reporter ion channel for the original chromatography protocol (Table S1). Most of the quantified proteins were RPs. This number of proteins is far below what was identified by other methods used to characterize RAPs, which suggests the original chromatography protocol, while suitable to purify the core ribosome, cannot be used to comprehensively identify RAPs.

We found that sucrose cushion pellet material binds to and elutes from the cysteine-charged resin. Importantly, poly(A)-enriched RNA does not bind to the resin (Figure 1B). When mESC lysate underwent the cysteine-charged resin chromatography, the flowthrough, which contains the RNP complexes that do not bind to the resin, overwhelmingly was composed of small, non-ribosomal RNPs, while the eluate contained a greater proportion of ribosomes (Figure S1B). This suggests the cysteinecharged resin preferentially binds ribosomes instead of small RNPs. Performing the entire RAPIDASH protocol eliminates small RNPs but retains ribosomes (Figure S1B).

Next, we asked whether the binding of ribosomes to the resin depends on the nascent polypeptide chain, mRNA, or RAPs. We isolated 80S ribosomes from untreated samples or samples treated with puromycin to release nascent polypeptide chains 18 ; RNase A to digest single-stranded RNAs 19-21 ; or a high concentration of ammonium chloride to remove RAPs 22 (Figure S1C). The RNase A-treated sample was probed by western blotting to confirm the loss of Pabp1, a protein that depends on mRNA to associate with the ribosome, and to test for the presence of MetAP1, which binds the ribosome independently of mRNA 1,23,24 (Figure 1C). The ammonium chloride-treated sample was analyzed by western blotting to confirm the loss of the salt-sensitive RAPs Naa15 and MetAP1 (Figure S1D). We then performed chromatography with these samples and quantified the RNA (80S ribosomes) that either remained bound to the cysteine-charged resin or was eluted. In all samples, the amount of RNA in the flowthrough samples was not detectable, which indicates they successfully bound to the cysteine-charged resin. The untreated, puromycin-treated, and RNase A-treated samples showed similar amounts of RNA that either remained bound to or were eluted from the resin (Figure 1C). This suggests that the presence of polypeptide chains or mRNA does not affect the binding of ribosomes to the cysteine-charged resin. However, the combined amount of eluted RNA and RNA that remained bound to the resin for the ammonium chloride-treated 80S ribosomes was lower relative to that of untreated 80S ribosomes (Figure S1E). One possible explanation for this is that stripping off RAPs exposes more rRNA, which causes the ribosomes to bind more strongly to the cysteine-charged resin and consequently be more difficult to elute.

Next, we asked whether the cysteine-charged resin chromatography differentially enriches ribosomes translating a subset of mRNAs. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) showed that less than

7% of genes change significantly (adjusted p &lt; 0.05, absolute fold change [FC] R 2) between samples that underwent the entire RAPIDASH protocol versus sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation alone (Figures S2A-S2C). This suggests the cysteinecharged resin chromatography is largely unbiased in terms of which mRNA-engaged ribosomes bind and elute.

We used TMT-MS to assess whether the cysteine-charged resin chromatography step allows for further enrichment of RPs compared with only performing sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation (Figure S3A). Figure 1D shows that compared with sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation alone, RAPIDASH significantly enriches RPs over other proteins. Although there might be a slight bias for 60S (large subunit) RPs over 40S (small subunit) RPs, this is not significant across all three replicates (Figure S3B).

We then asked whether non-ribosomal, large protein complexes are depleted after the entire RAPIDASH protocol compared with sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation alone. We ranked the proteins by their average enrichment ratios of RAPIDASH compared with sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation across three replicates and asked which Gene Ontology (GO) cellular component (GOCC) terms are enriched for proteins that are in the bottom 10%. The most significantly enriched terms for these proteins were related to mitochondria, nucleus, cytoskeleton, and vesicles (Figure S3C; Table S2). To confirm the depletion of non-ribosomal, large complexes and structures by RAPIDASH, we compared ultracentrifugation pellets versus RAPIDASH eluate samples for components of these complexes. As expected, when approximately equal amounts of RPs were loaded for each sample (Figure S3D), the nuclear pore complex (Nup62), adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthase (Atp5a1), and the translocase of the outer membrane (TOM) complex (Tom20) were depleted in the RAPIDASH sample compared with the sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation pellet sample (Figure 1E). Subsequently, we assessed whether the RAPIDASH workflow can maintain interactions between ribosomes and their associated proteins. The known RAPs Ufl1, 5,8 Upf1, 25 Ddx1, 5 Nsun2, 5 and Metap1 1 were all present in mESC RAPIDASH samples (Figures 1F and S3E).

We next wanted to assess the sensitivity of RAPIDASH in capturing translation components. We detected 793 proteins present in at least 3 out of 6 replicates (Table S3, column O = 1). Figure 1G shows the comparison of proteins in translational machinery that are identified by Ribo-FLAG IP using Byonic (v2.12.0) 26 and analyzed by significance analysis of interactome (SAINT) (v2.5.0) 27 as described previously, 5 or in at least three out of six RAPIDASH samples identified using MaxQuant (v2.4.2.0). 28,29 RAPIDASH identified all RPs except for Uba52 (Rpl40) and Rpl41. Compared with Ribo-FLAG IP, RAPIDASH captured more translation elongation factors, initiation factors, and transfer RNA (tRNA) synthetases (Figure 1G). In addition, RAPIDASH detected 298 out of the 428 (70%) proteins identified by Ribo-FLAG IP 5 (Table S3, column P = 1), which suggests RAPIDASH yields a high-quality list of candidate RAPs. We also compared our mESC RAPIDASH data to the recent work of ARC-MS in human embryonic stem cells (hESCs). 7 Out of the 793 proteins identified in RAPIDASH MS samples, there are 380 proteins that were also identified by ARC-MS in hESCs (Table S3, column Q = 1). Lastly, we tested the ability of RAPIDASH to detect RAP interactions with ribosomes in a distinct cancer cell type. For this experiment, we used PC3 cells, an aggressive metastatic human prostate cancer cell line in which constitutive mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) hyperactivation is essential for its tumorigenic potential. 30 We used RAPIDASH to isolate ribosomes complexes from PC3 cells that had been grown for 24 h in the presence or absence of the mTOR inhibitor Rapalink-1 and performed TMT-MS (Table S4). Although mTORC1 controls RP abundance, we did not observe major differences in RPs in our cells at these early conditions upon Rapalink-1 treatment. However, TMT-MS detected select proteins with altered associations with ribosomes upon mTOR inhibition: eIF4G1 and G3BP2 (Table S4). The translation initiation factor eIF4G1 decreased association with ribosomes upon mTOR inhibition, consistent with work showing that eIF4G1 is sequestered away from the ribosome when mTOR is inactive, 31 and G3BP2 was recently shown to regulate mTOR signaling. 32 In addition, two other proteins, PA2G4 (Ebp1) and PDCD4, also changed their association with the ribosome upon mTOR inhibition but were just below the stringent FC cutoffs that we set (Table S4). PA2G4, which regulates proteostasis through interactions with the 60S exit tunnel, 33 also decreased association with ribosomes upon mTOR inhibition. The translation inhibitor PDCD4 34 increased ribosome association upon mTOR inhibition. The significance of the differential interactions of these RAPs with ribosomes in cancer cells and downstream of mTOR signaling remains an interesting area of exploration.

## Application of RAPIDASH to the developing mouse forebrain identifies Dhx30 as a RAP

We next sought to identify RAPs present in the developing mouse forebrain tissue. We performed RAPIDASH on E12.5 forebrains followed by LC-MS/MS. This analysis yielded 518

protein identifications present in 2 out of 3 replicates (Table S5, column L = 1). GO analysis 16 of the data showed significantly enriched GOMF terms such as RNA binding and mRNA binding (Figure 2A), suggesting that proteins identified are bona fide RAPs with potential forebrain-specific functions.

To identify hits for further functional characterization, we separated the identified proteins into two classes: (1) proteins that regulate translation but are not known ribosome binders and (I2) proteins that bind the ribosome but are not known to regulate translation. Following this schema, we identified Dhx30 as a paradigm class I protein. Dhx30 belongs to the DExH (AspGlu-X-His) class of RNA helicases. DExD/H-box RNA helicases are highly conserved enzymes that use the energy of ATP to remodel RNA secondary structure and are vital for regulating various aspects of the RNA life cycle that when perturbed can lead to disease. 35

Biallelic loss of Dhx30 leads to embryonic lethality in mice exhibiting distinct early neural defects. 36 Strikingly, mutations mapped to the helicase and RNA-binding domains of human DHX30 have been directly identified in pediatric neurodevelopmental disease resulting in gait abnormalities or inability to walk, speech impairment, and severe intellectual disability. 37,38 Dhx30 has been implicated in cytoplasmic translational regulation and has been suggested to bind to mRNAs that carry a 3 0 UTR CG-rich motif mediating p53-dependent death (CGPD-motif) and decrease their translation. 39

We observed that Dhx30 co-fractionates with ribosomal subunits, mature 80S ribosomes, and polysomes (Figure 2B). Furthermore, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-mediated dissociation of mature 80S ribosomes and polysomes disrupts the Dhx30 co-fractionation profile along the gradient (Figure 2C). Our findings further indicate Dhx30 interaction with the ribosome is mRNA-independent, as Dhx30 still co-fractionates with 80S ribosomes after RNase A treatment (Figure 2D). Knockdown of Dhx30 with small interfering RNA (siRNA) did not significantly change the global protein synthesis rate (Figure 2E). These data support prior literature describing Dhx30 mediation of translation of specific subsets of mRNAs. 39,40

Dhx30 has two oligonucleotide/oligosaccharide binding (OB)fold domains, two double-stranded RNA-binding domains (dsRBDs), a core of two helicase domains, and a helicase-associated domain (HA2) 37 (Figure 2F). To test which domain is involved in ribosome interactions, we generated a variety of V5-tagged Dhx30 mutants that harbor either human disease-relevant

mutations that inactivate helicase activity (R805/8A) 37 or domain truncations (Figure 2F) and transfected them into mESCs. Although the R805/8A mutant expressed at a lower level compared with wild-type Dhx30 and thus may be less stable, we observed that whatever R805/8A protein was expressed was enriched in the pellet compared with the supernatant, which suggests helicase activity is not required for ribosome binding (Figures 2G and 2H). Although the D OB-fold mutant also expressed at a lower level and thus may also be less stable, the protein that was present did not associate with the pellet (Figures 2G and 2H), which suggests the D OB-fold may play an important role in Dhx30 stability, ribosome association, or both. Additionally, we observed almost complete loss of ribosome association in samples that expressed the D dsRBD construct (Figure 2G). Taken together, these data suggest Dhx30 may bind the ribosome through its OB-fold and dsRBDs. These findings thereby identify a bona fide RAP from our RAPIDASH results and begin to delineate the domains of Dhx30 that bind to the ribosome.

## LLPH as a RAP is important for neurodevelopment

Our forebrain data also yielded a paradigm class II protein called Llph. The homolog, LAPS18, was first functionally characterized in Limax marginatus , where it was suggested to be a secreted protein important for long-term memory formation. 41 By contrast, work studying the Aplysia kurodai homolog Aplysia LAPS18-like protein (ApLLP) described ApLLP as a nuclear protein enriched in the nucleolus that is important for the switch to long-term facilitation. 42,43 In mice, Llph is highly expressed in the developing mouse brain, but its expression wanes once the pup is born. Further work studying mouse Llph characterized it as a cellpermeable protein that is important for neural development. 44 Our RAPIDASH data suggest Llph may link the ribosome to a critical role in neural development and function.

Recently, cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the pre-60S particle in yeast 45 and humans 46 have captured the N terminus of LLPH binding to the ribosome at the base of sarcin-ricin loop (Figure 3A), a highly conserved sequence critical for GTP hydrolysis of EF1A and EEF2, which enables proper translocation during protein synthesis. 47,48 Therefore, LLPH may be important for regulating translation elongation.

First, we confirmed LLPH is a RAP. Because LLPH is easier to detect in human samples than mouse samples, we used P493-6 lymphoblastoid cells, which express LLPH at a high level. P493-6 cell lysate was fractionated, and the fractions were probed by western blotting for the presence of LLPH (Figures 3B and S4A). This showed LLPH associates mainly with the 60S subunit but is also present in mature ribosomes and polysomes. Treat- ment of the lysate with EDTA or RNase A shows that LLPH comigrates with the 60S subunit (Figures 3B and S4A).

We next established an A549 cell line that expresses LLPHFLAG when treated with doxycycline. IP of LLPH-FLAG from these cells following doxycycline treatment and analysis by MS revealed LLPH binds to the ribosome. Additional binding partners, which include the elongation factor EEF2; the RAPs PA2G4, SERBP1, and CCDC124; the RTRAF-RTCB-DDX1FAM98B complex; and the BTF3-NACA complex, hint at a possible role in translational control (Figure S4B; Table S6). For example, EEF2 is involved with ribosome translocation. 49 PA2G4, as stated above, is an RNA-binding protein that binds near the exit tunnel on the 60S subunit that regulates proteostasis 33 and is also involved in growth regulation. 50-52 SERBP1 and CCDC124 bind to hibernating ribosomes and help the recovery of translation. 53 The RTRAF-RTCB-DDX1-FAM98B complex binds to the cap of the mRNA and increases the rate of translation. 54 The BTF3-NACA binds to the nascent polypeptide and prevents non-ER proteins from being trafficked to the ER. 55,56 These data suggest that LLPH may participate in different steps of translational control.

Next, we wanted to directly characterize LLPH's role as a RAP in neurons. CRISPR-Cas9 was used to edit LLPH in H1-hESCs so that only the N-terminal 24 amino acids of LLPH, plus five extra amino acids that were the result of the genomic editing, were expressed (LLPH Nterm/Nterm ) (Figure S4C). Importantly, LLPH Nterm/Nterm hESCs are indistinguishable from wild-type hESCs in terms of growth (Figure S4D). We differentiated these hESCs into induced neurons (hiNs). 57 Our data reveal LLPH binds to the ribosome in an RNase-independent manner (Figure S4E). We assessed the morphology of the hiNs and observed that LLPH Nterm/Nterm neurons form shorter neurites with fewer secondary and tertiary neurites compared with the wild-type control neurons (Figures 3C and 3D).

Wenext carried out ribosome profiling at an early stage during differentiation (days in vitro [DIV] 14) to determine whether a difference in translation preceded the morphological differences (Figure 3E; Table S7). Genes that were translationally downregulated in LLPH Nterm/Nterm mutant hiNs included those involved with the extracellular matrix (ECM), such as a cadherin and two protocadherins and CDH5, PCDHA10, and PCDH5B; procollagen C-endopeptidase enhancer 2 (PCOLCE2), 58 and the guanine exchange factor RAPGEF6, which has been linked to neuritogenesis 59 (Figure 3F). This is striking because the ECM is important for many aspects of neurodevelopment. 60,61 In addition, several non-ECM genes important for neurodevelopment are also translationally downregulated in the LLPH Nterm/Nterm mutant hINs compared with wild-type hINs, such as CSPG4, 62 MYORG, 63

RHOBTB2, 64,65 SEMA3A, 66 and TTC23 67,68 (Figure 3F). These may help explain why LLPH Nterm/Nterm hiNs fail to produce neurites of the same length as wild-type hiNs.

Finally, because the binding site of LLPH on the ribosome suggests a role in regulating elongation, we asked whether genes that are translationally downregulated in LLPH Nterm/Nterm hiNs have any correlation with length since elongation rate is known to be negatively correlated with coding sequence length. 69 We strikingly found translationally downregulated genes are enriched for those with longer coding sequences compared with those that show no change in translation when comparing LLPH Nterm/Nterm and wild-type LLPH hiNs, which suggests LLPH may have a role in promoting elongation (Figures 3G and S4F). This is intriguing in the context of neurons because neuronal genes can have long coding sequences. Since coding sequence length also negatively correlates with translation fidelity, 70 regulation of elongation may be crucial to faithfully translate neuronal genes.

## Identification and characterization of tissue-specific RAPs in developing mouse embryo

Wenext asked whether RAPs change across embryonic tissues, specifically E12.5 limbs, forebrain, and liver, by using TMT-MS (Figure 4A).

Wedetected 566 proteins across all four replicates of three tissues (Table S8). Proteins that had FC R 2 or a false discovery rate (FDR) &lt; 0.1 when comparing one tissue to another were considered enriched in a particular tissue. Comparing forebrain and limb samples to liver samples yielded more proteins that passed the significance cutoffs than for forebrain versus limb samples (Figure 4B). This might be due to the more homogeneous cell composition in liver tissue compared with the other tissues, which highlights the inter-tissue differences. Hemoglobin subunit alpha (Hba) was shown to be enriched in the liver as a RAP compared with the other two tissues, which hints at a previously described role for the ribosome's role in heme bioavailability regulation. 71 Hkdc1, a kinase that phosphorylates hexose to hexose-6-phosphate in glycolysis, is enriched as a RAP in the liver relative to the other two tissues. This finding is reminiscent of how Pkm, another metabolic kinase, was shown to directly interact with ER ribosomes and regulate translation of specific mRNAs. 5

It has also been observed to bind to ribosomes in vitro , which suggests vimentin may help anchor ribosomes in the complex spatial architecture of neurons. 75

Strikingly, we also identified the secreted growth factors midkine (Mdk) and pleiotrophin (Ptn) as putative RAPs across all three tissues, although they were both enriched in the forebrain relative to the liver (Figure 4B). Their receptor is a brain-specific transmembrane protein tyrosine phosphatase Ptprz1, 76 which was also found to be a forebrain-enriched RAP. All three of these proteins are involved in neural development. It is tempting to speculate that the ribosome is a substrate for Ptprz1, and the binding of the ligands Mdk or Ptn may modulate phosphatase activity on the ribosome and initiate a translational program that is important for neural development.

Finally, Elavl2 is an RNA-binding protein that is a forebrain-enriched RAP compared with both liver and limbs. Elavl2 is a paradigm class I protein; although it is an RNA-binding protein that has been suggested to be a translation repressor in ovaries, 77 it is possible that in these embryonic tissues, Elavl2 is also a direct ribosome binder. Elavl2 was more enriched in the ribosome fractions in the forebrain compared with other tissues (Figure 4C), which suggests its role on the ribosome may be important for brain development. Taken together, RAPIDASH has revealed hundreds of potential RAPs in embryonic tissues that may play a role in tissue-specific functions.

## RAPIDASH reveals dynamic ribosome composition remodeling during macrophage stimulation

Finally, we leveraged RAPIDASH to understand how an acute stimulus could temporally remodel ribosome complexes within a given cell type. Mechanistically, macrophages sense the presence of bacterial and viral pathogens via germline-encoded pattern recognition receptors. 78 For example, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) senses invasion by gram-negative bacteria via binding to their outer cell membrane component lipopolysaccharide (LPS). At the same time, TLR3 alerts the macrophage to potential viral infection via binding to double-stranded RNA.

The forebrain and limbs had very few RAPs that were differentially enriched; all RAPs that were differentially enriched between these two tissues were enriched in the forebrain relative to the limbs. Creatine kinase b (Ckb), a kinase important for energy transduction in energy-intensive tissues (e.g., muscle, heart, and brain), 72 is enriched as a RAP in the forebrain over both the liver and limbs. We next performed TMT-MS on RAPIDASH samples from BMDMs 0, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h post-activation by the TLR3 agonist polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C)). We found that ribosome complexes were gradually remodeled over time (Figure 5A; Table S10). By 24 h post-stimulation, 410 proteins were enriched 2-fold, and 50 proteins were de-enriched 2-fold compared with ribosome complexes in resting BMDMs. These included the interferon (IFN)-induced protein with tetratricopeptide (IFIT) repeats proteins IFIT1, IFIT2, and IFIT3, which inhibit translation of viral transcripts 79 ; the ubiquitin-like protein ISG15, which is co-translationally ligated onto viral proteins thus interfering with their function 80 ; and the E3 ubiquitin ligase for ISG15, Herc6. 81,82 We also identified the deubiquitylase USP15, which associates with polysomes and may stabilize newly synthesized (viral) proteins, 83,84 and the prolyl hydroxylase P4HA1, which has been demonstrated to co-translationally modify proline residues of flavivirus proteins. 85 Among the 59 proteins ''ejected'' from ribosome complexes upon TLR3 stimulation, we identified PDCD4,which,aswenoted above, is a known ribosome-interacting translational inhibitor. 86 We also identified HMGB1, HMGB2, and HMGB3 proteins; HMGB2 in particular has recently been implicated in ribosome biogenesis 87 and has been identified as a putative RAP. 88 The function of this family of proteins on resting ribosome complexes remains to be explored.

Intriguingly, among RAPs that were highly enriched in TLR3stimulated macrophages, we identified multiple cytoplasmic RNA sensors, such as members of the 2 0 ,5 0 -oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS) family that are known to regulate translation in the context of viral infection but have not been previously identified as RAPs. It is tempting to speculate that these findings indicate a mode of on-ribosome sensing of immunostimulatory viral RNAs (which may, for example, provide a mechanism of selectively degrading ''infected'' ribosomes via local RNase L activation). Indeed, published work has confirmed the ability of one sensor, retinoic-acid inducible gene I (RIG-I), to localize to ribosomes via binding to an rRNA ES. 89

In addition to RNA sensors, we identified several other IFNstimulated RAPs in TLR3-activated macrophages. Among them, Viperin (encoded by the gene Rsad2 ) and CMPK2 have both recently been implicated in translational regulation. 90,91 Mechanistically, these proteins have been proposed to inhibit viral translation via induction of ribosome collisions. Strikingly, stimulation of BMDMs with LPS for 0, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h showed an overall remarkably similar effect on ribosome complexes as did activation with poly(I:C) (Figures 5B and S5B; Table S11). Of the 206 proteins enriched 2-fold 24 h after LPS stimulation, 111 were also 2-fold enriched, and an additional 20 were 1.5-fold enriched following

24hofpoly(I:C) stimulation. This similarity in ribosome remodeling may be because TLR3 and TLR4 signal through a shared downstream adapter protein, TIR (Toll/interleukin-1 [IL-1] receptor)domain-containing adapter-inducing interferonb (TRIF). The greater number of highly enriched RAPs identified following poly(I:C) stimulation (395) compared with LPS stimulation (198) mayreflect the more robust TRIF-dependent signaling and IFN induction that occurs downstream of TLR3 ligation.

We confirmed several hits common to TLR3- and TLR4-stimulated macrophages as bona fide RAPs (Figures 5C-5F). Lastly, we did identify several intriguing RAPs that were selectively enriched following LPS treatment. These included the proteins caspase recruitment domain-containing protein 9 (CARD9 and SON DNA-binding protein (SON), which have known roles in immune defense 92,93 and been putatively identified as RAPs. 88

Therefore, RAPIDASH has enabled the characterization of dynamic changes to ribosomes upon macrophage stimulation.

## DISCUSSION

## RAPIDASH can characterize RAPs in different sample types

We have developed a method called RAPIDASH that can enrich ribosomes and RAPs. The specificity of RAPIDASH is largely due to the cysteine-charged sulfolink resin chromatography step. Although the cysteine-charged sulfolink resin preferentially binds to rRNA compared with poly(A) RNA, the mechanism by which this specificity is achieved is unclear and could potentially be due to an affinity for structured RNA or RNA modifications that are present in rRNA.

Compared with Ribo-FLAG IP, RAPIDASH can be used on any sample, requires less input material, and has better coverage. Webelieve RAPIDASH can be used for many other cell types, tissues, organisms, and dynamic conditions, including clinical settings and non-model organisms. RAPIDASH is capable of processing low-input samples. Based on our proteomic data, a single E12.5 limb bud should yield on average about 350 ng of RAPs. In addition, while RAPIDASH can generate a decent-quality list of candidate RAPs, it is important to validate that these proteins are true hits, for example, by sucrose gradient fractionation of samples treated with EDTA.

## Implications of RAPs with tissue-specific functions and RAPs during immune activation

Our findings in tissues suggest that RAPs may enable the ribosome to coordinate tissue-specific functions. For example, the helicase function of Dhx30 may regulate the translational specificity of the ribosome in neurons by denaturing secondary structures of select mRNAs. It would be interesting to identify the

targets of Dhx30 and how their translational efficiency changes if Dhx30 carries the same mutations as in patients. Our data suggest LLPH may enable the proficient translation of neural developmental genes with long coding sequences, including those that are required for neurite outgrowth, which reveals a potential RAP-mediated mechanism for neural translation.

Wehave also identified various RAPs that could play a tissuespecific role in normal mouse development. Elavl2 is capable of binding both ribosome and mRNA; it remains to be seen precisely how this impacts translation of Elavl2 binding targets. Interestingly, we see cytoskeleton-related components such as Vimentin and Map1b being enriched in the forebrain. Other cytoskeletal components have previously been found to be associated with ribosomes, which hints at possible roles in localized translation. 94-97 Map1b has been shown to play a role in microtubule remodeling critical for axonal outgrowth. 98 Possibly, ribosomes are recruited alongside Map1b to distal growth cones, or facilitate central sprouting and peripheral regeneration of neurons. 98-100 Additionally, the identification of Ckb and Ptprz1, a kinase and phosphatase, respectively, as potential RAPs suggests they enable rapid translational responses to cell signals. Finally, we have applied RAPIDASH to stimulated macrophages over time and observed extensive changes in ribosome composition. These data suggest that pathogen detection leads to a translational rewiring to help macrophages overcome infection. Notably, activation of TLR3 and TLR4 leads to overlapping changes in ribosome composition, which suggests the usage of common mechanisms of translational control despite distinctions in inflammatory contexts.

## Outlook

Thus, RAPIDASH has revealed a dynamic and diverse layer of ribosome composition that is important for tissue-specific functions and responses to acute stimuli, such as in macrophages and cancer cells. In the future, RAPIDASH can be used to characterize ribosome composition in a variety of samples to characterize how the ribosome acts as a cell signal integrator to regulate gene expression in health and disease.

## Limitations of the study

While RAPIDASH is a versatile technique that can analyze ribosome composition across different cells, tissues, and organisms, there are some limitations. First, every protein identified by RAPIDASH is a candidate RAP. The cutoffs applied to the proteomic data in this manuscript were arbitrarily chosen and are fairly stringent; thus, there may be biologically relevant RAPs that fall below these cutoffs. Moreover, as stated above, every candidate RAP must be validated by orthogonal techniques, such as sucrose gradient fractionation experiments. Second, the lower limit of input required for MS-based proteomic analysis of RAPIDASH eluate has not been fully characterized and may present a barrier for certain sample types where the amount of input is extremely limiting. Finally, RAPs identified by RAPIDASH may not be exclusively localized to the ribosome, so it is important to carefully dissect a protein's function on the ribosome versus other extra-ribosomal functions.

## Chromatography with cysteine-charged resin

To perform the cysteine-charged sulfolink characterization, sucrose cushion pellet and poly(A) samples were thawed on ice, and the RNAconcentrations were measured using a Qubit RNA high sensitivity (HS) assay kit. First, 500 m L Sulfolink coupling resin (Thermo Fisher, 20402) was added to a falcon tube and centrifuged at 850 3 g for 1 minute at room temperature, and the supernatant was decanted. The resin was then resuspended in 1 resin volume (e.g., 500 m L) coupling buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5 (Fisher Scientific, BP153-500), 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Invitrogen, AM9262)), and then centrifuged at 850 3 g for 1 minute at room temperature. The supernatant was decanted, and the resin was washed twice more with 1 volume (i.e., 500 m L) coupling buffer as previously described. After the last wash was decanted, the resin was resuspended in 1 volume (i.e., 500 m L) 50 mM L-cysteine (Sigma Aldrich, 168149-25G) in coupling buffer and left on a rocker at room temperature for at least one hour. Then, the resin was centrifuged at 850 3 g for 1 minute at room temperature. The supernatant wasdecanted, and then the resin was resuspended in 1 volume (i.e., 500 m L) coupling buffer and centrifuged at 850 3 g for 1 minute at roomtemperature. The supernatant was decanted, and this washing step was repeated twice more. The resin was then washed three times with 2 volumes (i.e., 1 mL) priming buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.6, 15 mM Mg(OAc)2, 60 mM NH4Cl, 1 mM DTT, and 100 m g/mL CHX) by resuspending the resin in the priming buffer, centrifuging at 850 3 g for 1 minute, and decanting the supernatant. The wash with priming buffer was repeated twice more, and then the resin was resuspended in 1 volume (i.e., 500 m L) priming buffer. For each sample, 12.5 m Lresin slurry was added to a micro-spin column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 89879), which was placed in a DNA lo-bind tube. The caps were removed. The micro-spin columns were centrifuged at 1,000 3 g for 1 minute at room temperature to remove the priming buffer from the resin, and then the bottom caps were put on again.

For each sucrose cushion pellet or poly(A) RNA sample, 42 - 48 m L was added to the resin for a total of 190 - 339 ng of RNA. The top caps were screwed on, and the columns were briefly vortexed to mix. The capped columns were put in DNA lo-bind tubes and put on ice for 15 minutes under foil. Then, the caps were removed, the columns were moved to fresh DNA lo-bind tubes, and the samples were centrifuged at 1,000 3 g for 1 minute at room temperature. The bottom caps were replaced, the flowthroughs were re-applied to the resin, and the top caps were screwed on. The columns were again briefly vortexed to mix, and then they were returned to the DNA lo-bind tubes and left on ice for 15 minutes under foil. Then, the caps were removed, and the samples were centrifuged at 1,000 3 g for 1 minute at room temperature. The flowthroughs were kept on ice, and the bottom caps were put on to the columns, which were moved to fresh DNA lo-bind tubes. The resin was then washed by adding 1 volume, (i.e., 12.5 m L) priming buffer to each column, capping the columns, vortexing the columns briefly to mix, uncapping the columns and returning them to their DNA lo-bind tubes, and centrifuging the samples at 1,000 3 g for 1 minute at room temperature. for a total of four washes. The bottom caps of the columns were put on, and the columns were moved to fresh DNA-lo bind tubes. ## Preparation of L-cysteine charged sulfhydryl resin

The amount of L-cysteine charged sulfhydryl resin required for each experiment was prepared while the samples were undergoing sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation. Per sample, 1 mL of SulfoLink /C212 coupling resin slurry was pipetted into a polypropylene tube (Corning, 352059), which was used in order to minimize resin loss during decanting. The slurry was initially centrifuged at 800 3 g for 1 minute at 4 /C14 C to pellet the resin, and the storage solution was decanted. The resin was then washed three times by adding a volume of cold coupling buffer equal to twice the bed volume, resuspending the resin by inverting gently, centrifuging at 800 3 g for 1 minute at 4 /C14 C, and decanting the supernatant after each centrifugation. Afterwards, a volume of 50 mM L-cysteine in coupling buffer equal to twice the bed volume was added to the resin. The tube was wrapped in aluminum foil and then rocked for 1 hour on a platform rocker at room temperature. The resin was then centrifuged at 800 3 g for 1 minute at 4 /C14 C, and the supernatant was decanted to collect the L-cysteine charged sulfhydryl resin. The charged resin was washed three times with cold priming buffer. For each

wash, cold priming buffer with four times the bed volume was added to the resin, which was resuspended by gently inverting the tube and then collected by centrifuging at 800 3 gfor 1 minute at 4 /C14 Canddecanting the supernatant. After the final wash, the charged resin was resuspended in a volume of the cold priming buffer equal to twice the bed volume, 1.5 mL charged resin slurry was aliquoted into one 5 mL spin column (Thermo Scientific, 89897) for each sample. Charged resin can be stored overnight at 4 /C14 C.

After the sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation step, the supernatant was removed by pipetting with a P1000 micropipette without disturbing the pellet, which contains high density protein complexes. Each pellet was dislodged with a pipette tip and resuspended in 1 mL of cold binding buffer by pipetting for 30 seconds. The tube was then sealed with parafilm wrap and shaken on a thermomixer at 1,000 rpm for 30 minutes at 4 /C14 C to further resuspend the protein. Immediately prior to incubation, the twist-off bottoms of the spin columns containing the charged resin were snapped off, and the columns were placed in 15 mL falcon tubes. The columns were then centrifuged at 1,000 3 g for 1 minute at 4 /C14 C to remove the priming buffer and subsequently capped with the provided bottom closures. The priming buffer was discarded. Then, for each sample, half of the resuspended protein pellet was transferred into a charged resin-containing spin column. The other half of each sample was retained to analyze the sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation step. The spin columns were flicked several times to mix and then incubated on ice for 15 minutes under foil. The bottom closures were then removed, and the flowthroughs were collected by centrifuging at 1,000 3 g for 1 minute at 4 /C14 C. ## Trypsin/Lys-C digestion

Each protein sample was solubilized by pipetting with a low retention pipette tip (Fisher Scientific, 02-717-135) in 50 m L denaturing buffer (50 mM NH4HCO3 (Sigma Aldrich, 09830-500G), 6 M urea (Sigma Aldrich, U1250-1KG), in HPLC water). The protein concentration for each sample was then quantified using a Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, 5000006) with technical duplicates following the manufacturer's protocol. For each mESC RAPIDASH sample (Figures 1G and 1H), 80 m g protein was denatured and reduced by adding 0.5 m L 500 mM DTT to each sample for a final concentration of 5 mM and incubating in a thermomixer at 37 /C14 C for 1 hour at 500 rpm. The samples were alkylated by adding 1 m L 500 mM iodoacetamide (Pierce, A39271) to each 50 m L sample for a final concentration of 15 mM and incubating them in the dark at room temperature for 30 minutes. A 0.2 m g/ m L Trypsin/Lys-C stock solution in Resuspension Buffer (Promega, V5073) was subsequently added to each sample at a 1:50 trypsin/Lys-C:sample ratio by mass to digest the protein into tryptic peptides. The samples were mixed and then shaken at 500 rpm for 4 hours in a thermocycler at 37 /C14 C for the initial digestion by Lys-C. Afterwards, each sample was diluted sixfold by adding 250 m L 50 mM NH4HCO3 to reduce the urea concentration to 1 M to allow trypsin to refold. The samples were then incubated for another 12 hours in a 37 /C14 C water bath to complete the digestion. Trypsinization was then quenched by adding 3 m L 50% (v/v) heptafluorobutyric acid (HFBA; Sigma Aldrich, 52411-5ML-F) in HPLC water to each sample. The samples were then either stored at -80 /C14 C or immediately desalted.

## Peptide desalting

Each sample was desalted using an OMIX C18 pipette tip (Agilent technologies, A57003100) attached to a P200 pipette set to 150 m L. Each OMIX C18 pipette tip was conditioned by pipetting 50% (v/v) acetonitrile (Fisher Scientific, A955-1) up and down three times. No air was allowed to pass through the sorbent after it was conditioned. The OMIX C18 pipette tip was then equilibrated with 1% (v/v) HFBA by pipetting up and down three times. Acidified peptides were bound to the OMIX C18 sorbent by pipetting up and down for seven times carefully. After the peptides were bound, each tip was rinsed with 0.1% (v/v) HFBA by pipetting up and down three times. To elute the peptides, 7 m L 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (Fisher Scientific, A117-50) in 50% (v/v) acetonitrile by carefully pipetting up and down five times. A second elution was performed by pipetting up and down with 7 m L 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in 75% (v/v) acetonitrile five times. The two elutions were then pooled together and dried completely using a speed-vac ( /C24 45 minutes). The dried peptide samples were stored at -80 /C14 C. The peptides were resuspended in 15 m L 0.1% formic acid for analysis by mass spectrometry.

## Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry

Massspectrometry was performed on an Acquity UPLC (ultra performance liquid chromatography) M-class system (Waters) coupled online to an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated by reversed-phase chromatography using a Self-Pack PicoFrit column (New Objective, PF360-75-15-N-5) with a 360 m m outer diameter, 75 m m inner diameter, and a tip size of 15 m mpacked to approximately 22 cm with HALO Peptide ES-C18 Bulk Packing 2.7 m mbeads (MAC-MOD Analytical, 942120202). The UPLC solvents A and B were 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and 0.1% formic acid (v/v) in/100% acetonitrile, respectively. For each sample, 2 m L was loaded at 1% B at 0.3 m L/minute for 20 minutes. Peptides were then separated at 0.3 m L/minute over a linear gradient from 1% B to 40% B for 90 minutes, followed by a linear gradient from 40% B to 100% B for 20 minutes, followed by a constant flow at 100% B for 10 minutes. Then, there was a linear ramp back down to 1% B over 5 minutes and then constant flow at 1% B for 5 minutes.

The Orbitrap Elite was operated in a data-dependent mode using Xcalibur v3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Collision-induced dissociation (CID) MS/MS scans were recorded after each MS1 scan (R = 120,000) for the top 15 most abundant precursor ions in the Orbitrap. The isolation width was 1.8 m/z, the normalized collision energy was 35%, and the activation time was 10 milliseconds. The dynamic exclusion parameters were: a repeat count 1 with a 45 second repeat duration, an exclusion list size of 500, an exclusion duration of 80 seconds, and a +/- 10 ppm exclusion mass width. Only charge states of 2 or 3 were not rejected; others, including unassigned charges, were rejected.

## Mass spectrometry analysis

Peptide digests resuspended in 0.1% formic acid were injected (approximately 2 m g) onto a 2 m m75 m mx50cmPepMapRSLCC18 EASY-Spray /C212 column (Thermo Scientific, ES903). For peptide elution, 3-hour water/acetonitrile gradients (2-25% in 0.1% formic acid) were used, at a flow rate of 200 nL/minute. Analysis was performed in an Orbitrap Eclipse (Thermo Scientific) in positive ion mode. MS spectra were acquired between 375 and 1500 m/z with a resolution of 120000. For each MS spectrum, multiply charged ions over the selected threshold (2E4) were selected for MS/MS in cycles of 3 seconds with an isolation window of 0.7 m/z. Precursor ions were fragmented by higher energy collision dissociation (HCD) using stepped relative collision energies of 30, 35 and 40 to ensure efficient generation of sequence ions as well as TMT reporter ions. MS/MS spectra were acquired in centroid mode with resolution 60000 from m/z=120. A dynamic exclusion window was applied which prevented the same m/z from being selected for 30 seconds after its acquisition.

## Peptide and protein identification and quantitation

Peak lists were generated using PAVA in-house software. 120 All generated peak lists were searched against the human subset of the SwissProt database (SwissProt.2019.07.31, 20432 entries searched), using Protein Prospector 107 with the following parameters: enzyme specificity was set as Trypsin, and up to 2 missed cleavages per peptide were allowed. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues, and TMTsixplex labeling of lysine residues and N-terminus of the protein, were allowed as fixed modifications. N-acetylation of the N-terminus of the protein, loss of protein N-terminal methionine, pyroglutamate formation from peptide N-terminal glutamines, oxidation of methionine were allowed as variable modifications. Mass tolerance was 10 ppm in MS and 30 ppm in MS/MS. The false positive rate was estimated by searching the data using a concatenated database which contains the original SwissProt database, as well as a version of each original entry where the sequence has been randomized. A 1% FDR was permitted at the protein and peptide level. For quantitation only unique peptides were considered; peptides common to several proteins were not used for quantitative analysis. For TMT based quantitation, relative quantization of peptide abundance was performed via calculation of the intensity of reporter ions corresponding to the different TMT labels, present in MS/MS spectra. Intensities were determined by Protein Prospector. Median intensities of the reporter ions (each TMT channel) for all peptide spectral matches (PSMs) were used to normalize individual (sample specific) intensity values. For each PSM, relative abundances were calculated as ratios vs the average intensity levels in the 3 channels corresponding to control (non-stimulated) samples. For total protein relative levels, peptide ratios were aggregated to the protein levels using median values of the log2 ratios. Significance was calculated using Student's t-test.

## TMT labeling

TMTsixplex /C212 Isobaric Label Reagents (Thermo Scientific, 90066) were freshly prepared for each experiment by resuspending the entire 0.8 mg of labeling reagent in the tube with 100 m L 100% ethanol. ## Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry for TMT

Peptides were separated by reversed-phase chromatography using the same column as in ''Mass spectrometry of unlabeled tryptic digests - Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry.'' UPLC solvent A was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid, and UPLC solvent B was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid/100% acetonitrile, respectively. For each sample, 3 m L was injected and loaded for 30 minutes at 1% B at a flow rate of 0.3 m L/minute. Peptides were separated at the same flow rate over a linear gradient of 5% B to 40% B for 180 minutes, followed by a linear ramp to 100% B for 10 minutes, followed by constant flow at 100% B for 10 minutes. Finally, there was a ramp down to 1% B over 1 minute, where it was held for 9 minutes at constant flow.

The Orbitrap Elite (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was operated in a data-dependent mode using Xcalibur v3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). HCD MS/MS scans (R = 15,000) were recorded after each MS1 scan (R = 60,000) for the top 15 most abundant precursor ions in the Orbitrap. The following HCD parameters were used: an isolation width of 1.6 m/z, a normalized collision energy of 40%, and an activation time of 0.1 milliseconds. Dynamic exclusion parameters were set as follows: a repeat count 1 with a 30 second repeat duration, an exclusion list size of 500, an exclusion duration of 60 seconds, and a +/- 10 ppm exclusion mass width. Charge state rejection was enabled for charge states that were less than 2 or unassigned.

## Mass spectrometry data analysis

Related to Figures 1D, 1G, 1H, 2A, 4B, S3B, and S3C. For identification, a minimum of one razor + unique peptide was required, and the result was filtered with 1% false discovery rate (FDR) at the peptide and protein levels. For TMT quantification, a minimum of two razor + unique peptides present was required instead.

For data related to Figures 1H (Table S3) and 2A (Table S5), proteins that were identified only by site, contaminants, and reverse hits were filtered out. For the TMT mass spectrometry data, contaminants, reverse hits, and peptides only identified by site were filtered out, and reporter intensity for each protein was log2 transformed and median-normalized. For the data related to Figures 1D and S3B (Table S2), only proteins that were detected in all three replicates were selected. Subsequently, the proteins were categorized to separate core RPs from other proteins. The significance of enriched protein groups in each replicate was evaluated with Welch's t-test. with a paired Welch's t-test using Perseus (v2.0.11) Proteins with a fold change R 2 and a permutation-based FDR &lt; 10% were deemed to be significantly enriched in one E12.5 mouse embryo tissue over another.

## Mass Spectrometry Analysis

Peptide digests were subjected to chromatographic separation using a 2 m m, 50 m mx150 mm, EASY-Spray C18 Reversed Phase HPLC column (Thermo Scientific, ES801). Water-acetonitrile gradients (2-25% in 0.1% formic acid over 60 minutes) were used to separate peptides at a flow rate of 200 nL/minute for analysis in an Orbitrap Lumos Fusion (Thermo Scientific) in positive ion mode. MS spectra were acquired between 375 and 1500 m/z with a resolution of 120000. For each MS spectrum, multiply charged ions over the selected threshold (2E4) were selected for MSMS in cycles of 3 seconds with an isolation window of 1.6 m/z. Precursor ions were fragmented by HCD using a relative collision energy of 30. MSMS spectra were acquired in centroid mode with resolution 30000 from m/z=110. A dynamic exclusion window was applied which prevented the same m/z from being selected for 30s after its acquisition.

## Peptide and protein identification and quantitation

Peak lists were generated using PAVA in-house software. 120 All generated peak lists were searched against the human subset of the SwissProt database (SwissProt.2019.07.31) using Protein Prospector. 107 The database search was performed with the following parameters: a mass tolerance of 10 ppm for precursor masses; 30 ppm for MS/MS, cysteine carbamidomethylation as a fixed modification and acetylation of the N terminus of the protein, pyroglutamate formation from N terminal glutamine, and oxidation of methionine as variable modifications. All spectra identified as matches to peptides of a given protein were reported, and the number of spectra (PSM) used for label free quantitation of protein abundance in the samples.

## Ribosome profiling analysis

Related to Figures 3E-3G. For Ribo-seq data from RFP samples, only analysis of Read 1 was necessary due to the short insert length. cutadapt version 2.4 110 was used to trim adapter sequences in both poly(A)-enriched RNA and RFP samples. For RFP samples, only sequences from Read 1 were trimmed from 3' adapter sequences with parameters ''-j 0 -u 3 -a AGATCGGAAGAGCACAGTCT GAACTCCAGTCAC -discard-untrimmed -m 15'', whereas in poly(A)-enriched RNA samples, both 3' and 5' adapters were trimmed from Read 1 and Read 2, respectively, with parameters ''-a AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCA -A AGATCGG AAGAGCGTCGTGTAGGGAAAGAGTGT''. For RFP samples, in-line barcodes were demultiplexed using fastx\_barcode\_splitter.pl (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx\_toolkit/) with parameters ''-eol''. Unique molecular identifiers and in-line barcodes were extracted using UMI-tools version 1.0.1 115 with parameters ''extract -extract-method=string -bc-pattern=NNNNNCCCCC --3prime''. Reads were filtered by quality using fastq\_quality\_filter (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx\_toolkit/) with parameters ''-Q33 -q 20 -p 70 -z''. GENCODE release 43 103 was used as a reference. To remove reads originating from rRNA, transfer RNA (tRNA), and small nuclear RNA(snRNA), reads aligning to these sequences using bowtie2 version 2.3.4.3 116 with parameters ''-L 18'' were discarded. PCR duplicates were then removed using UMI-tools. Ribosome A site positions were determined by offsetting the distance of the 5' end of each RFP read to canonical start sites in each length group and adding 4 nucleotides. Reads aligning to the CDS were used for RFP libraries, while reads aligning to the entire transcript were used for RNA-seq libraries. Transcript abundance for RNA-seq samples were quantified using salmon version 1.4.0. 113 Reads mapping to mitochondrial DNA genes were excluded from further analysis. Transcripts with counts per million (CPM) &gt;2 were retained for downstream analysis RFP and RNA-seq libraries were normalized separately by the trimmed mean of M-values method in edgeR. 117 Differential RFP and RNA-seq abundance and enrichment were analyzed using voom 119 and limma. 118 Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.
